II. APPLICABLE LAW
2
A pretrial motion to dismiss seeks dismissal on a question of law.  See United States v.
3
Schulman, 817 F.2d 1355, 1358 (9th Cir. 1987).  In ruling on such a motion, “the district court is
4
bound by the four corners of the indictment.”  United States v. Lyle, 742 F.3d 434, 436 (9th Cir.
5
2014); United States v. Boren, 278 F.3d 911, 914 (9th Cir. 2002) (“On a motion to dismiss an
6
indictment for failure to state an offense the court must accept the truth of the allegations in the
7
indictment in analyzing whether a cognizable offense has been charged.”).  The court asks only
8
whether a crime has been alleged.  United States v. Milovanovic, 678 F.3d 713, 717 (9th Cir.
9
2012).  Rule 12 motions cannot be used to determine “general issues of guilt or innocence.”
10
Boren, 278 F.3d at 914 (citation omitted).
11
Supra, defendant was charged with a violating 36 C.F.R. § 2.35(c).  The essential
12
elements of the regulation require defendant’s presence within Yosemite National Park, in a state
13
of intoxication, and in a position of vulnerability to danger.  See United States v. Regan, 93 F.
14
Supp. 2d 82, 86 (D. Mass. 2000).
15
III. ANALYSIS
16
The following facts are alleged in the affidavit in support of the complaint.  At 7:30 p.m.,
17
dispatch radioed emergency medical services regarding an “unresponsive male in the back of a
18
Yosemite Shuttle Bus.”  (Doc. 1, ¶ 6).  Defendant was “passed out” on the bus “for approximately
19
and hour and a half.”  (Id. at 6).  The first officer to arrive found defendant “slumped” on the bus
20
seat.  (Id.).  Defendant responded after he was “shook . . by the shoulder.”  (Id.).  Defendant
21
“smelled heavily of acholic beverages” and, when defendant was questioned about how much he
22
had to drink, he responded, “quite a bit.”  (Id., ¶ 7).  Although Defendant complied with the
23
ranger’s requests, he was “slow to respond.”  (Id.).  When standing, Defendant was “shaky and
24
unsteady,” and rangers had to place him in an “escort hold” to “keep him on his feet” and get him
25
to the door because he was “swaying” and “walking with an inconsistent gait.”  (Id., ¶ 8).  Once
26
off the bus, rangers had to “assist” defendant and “lean” him “against the push bumper” of the
27
patrol vehicle.  (Id., ¶ 9).  When questioned again about how much is “quite a bit,” defendant
28
responded, “he was just trying to get to the employee pub.”  (Id.).  When asked where his dorm

was, defendant again stated he intended to go to the Denegan’s Kitchen, which houses the

Swaying, walking unsteadily, and smelling of alcohol are suggestive of excessive alcohol
4
consumption.  Being "passed out" indicates a level of intoxication where the person cannot care
5
for their own safety, and being “unresponsive” on public transport (such as a park shuttle) can
6
constitute a safety risk to oneself, other passengers, and the operation of the vehicle.  For
7
instance, an individual who is “passed out” on a bus is vulnerable to crime and susceptible to
8
injury if the bus must swerve or make an abrupt stop.
9
The complaint therefore sufficiently alleges the essential elements of 36 C.F.R. §2.35(c)
10
and thus cannot be dismissed.  The cases cited by defendant, United States v. Nyemaster, 116
11
F.3d 827, 829 (9th Cir. 1997) and U.S. v. Hogue, 752 F.2d 1503 (9th Cir.1985), are
12
distinguishable.  The issue before the Ninth Circuit in each case was whether the evidence was
13
sufficient to support the finding of guilt after trial.  At a Rule 12(b) stage, the question before the
14
court is not one of plaintiff’s guilt or innocence, but instead a question of whether the complaint
15
outlines the elements and alleges sufficient facts for this court to find probable cause that
16
defendant has committed the charged offense.  Fed. R. Crim. P. 3.  Whether defendant is guilty of
17
the charged offense is a question of fact reserved for trial and is not proper in a pretrial motion.
18
